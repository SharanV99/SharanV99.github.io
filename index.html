04 / R · LASSO REGRESSION

Boston 311 Service Requests

Question
What actually drives how long the City of Boston takes to resolve a service request, and is the city getting faster?
Approach
Cleaned 1.3M service requests in R and engineered 31 features, including geospatial and temporal variables. Used LASSO regression to isolate the drivers of resolution time, with stepwise regression as a check.
Finding
Requests submitted through the City Worker App were resolved 284 hours faster than those from consumer-facing channels. Average response time improved 13% year over year (119 hours per year, p < 0.001).
Recommendation
Expand employee training and City Worker App adoption, since the internal channel is where the speed gains are.
03 / RAG · LANGCHAIN · KUBERNETES

AI Travel Concierge

TEAM PROJECT · NORTHEASTERNFALL 2025LLAMA 3 · GROQSTREAMLIT
Problem
The 2026 World Cup spanned 16 host cities across the USA, Canada, and Mexico. Fans with tickets often lacked the “last-mile” logistics: stadium transfer rules, safe neighborhoods, and transit routes. Generic travel sites didn’t have match-specific context, and standard AI chatbots tended to invent match dates and venues that didn’t exist.
What we built
An AI travel planner in Streamlit. Users pick a city, a match, and their interests, and a LangChain RAG pipeline with Llama 3 (via the Groq API) builds a day-by-day itinerary from a curated knowledge base of match schedules, stadium logistics, and city guides. Deployed with Docker and Kubernetes (Minikube), with ELK dashboards tracking user searches and API latency.
Challenge
Even with the schedule data available, the model kept planning trips around matches that didn’t exist, sometimes right after admitting the match wasn’t on the schedule.
How we solved it
A validation step checks every request against the official FIFA schedule before anything is generated. If the match isn’t real, the app stops and corrects the user (“France is playing in New York, not Boston”). Getting the model to reliably stop took many rounds of prompt iteration.

02 / PYTHON · CHURN

Last.fm Churn Propensity

SOLO PROJECTLAST.FM APISCIKIT-LEARNSTATSMODELS
Question
Can listening history predict which users are about to go silent on the platform, and which of them are actually worth contacting to win back?
Approach
Collected my own data from the Last.fm API: built a roster of about 900 real users by snowballing through the friends endpoint, then pulled each user’s listening history. Last.fm has no churn label, so I defined one: no plays in the last 45 days. Because churn is defined by recency, recency can’t be a feature; every feature comes from behavior before the 45-day window (listening volume, artist variety, gaps between sessions, and activity trend). Trained a logistic regression with balanced class weights (AUC 0.82).
Decision
Turned predictions into a targeting decision: for each user, expected value = churn probability × customer value × save rate − contact cost. Only users with positive expected value are contacted. The 18% save rate is benchmarked from a real telecom win-back campaign I ran at Sarada Technologies.
Finding
Of 823 analyzed users, 171 are worth a retention nudge. A power analysis showed a valid A/B test needs about 295 users (148 per arm) for 80% power, so the targetable group is too small to prove the nudge works; I flag that rather than over-read a result.
Limitations
The roster was snowballed from an older seed account, so it over-represents early adopters and inflates the churn rate (about 80%). Silence on Last.fm is a proxy: someone may have stopped scrobbling while still listening elsewhere. Customer values are illustrative; the expected-value framework is the contribution.

01 / PYTHON · SCIKIT-LEARN · XGBOOST

Predicting Hospitalization Intensity

SOLO · NORTHEASTERN CAPSTONEWINTER 2026SHAPSTREAMLIT
Problem
Hospital stays are among the costliest events in U.S. healthcare, and one admission is very different from repeated admissions: median spending was about 37 times higher for adults admitted two or more times. So instead of a yes/no model, this project classifies adults into None, Single, or Multiple hospitalizations.
What I built
Merged five Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) file types for Panels 6–10 (2001–2006): person, condition, prescription, inpatient, and ER files. Built chronic-condition flags from ICD-9 codes and a 26-feature set covering demographics, insurance, health status, and utilization, for 50,733 adults, excluding adults with cancer diagnoses. Compared elastic-net logistic regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost, and built a Streamlit dashboard to explore the results.
The catch
Total healthcare spending looked like a strong predictor, but it already contains the cost of the hospital stays being predicted. Removing it dropped Macro AUC by 0.13–0.16, showing how much of the apparent accuracy was leakage. All final models exclude it. I also compared SMOTE against class weights for the 53-to-1 class imbalance; class weights caught far more repeat-admission patients (62.9% vs 38.3% recall).
Results
No single model won every metric. Random Forest ranked risk best (Macro AUC 0.84), logistic regression caught the most repeat-admission patients (62.9% recall), and XGBoost was strongest on single stays (54.5% recall). Across all models, ER visits, number of medications, self-rated health, and age were the most consistent signals.
Limitations
Predictors and outcome come from the same survey year, so this is risk stratification rather than advance prediction; the next step is predicting Year 2 admissions from Year 1 data. Survey weights were not applied, the Multiple class had only 167 test cases, and results come from a single train/test split. About 70% of single-stay adults are female versus 54% overall, likely reflecting childbirth admissions; separating pregnancy-related stays from illness-related ones is a natural next step. The 2001–2006 data uses ICD-9 codes, so findings may not reflect current care patterns.
